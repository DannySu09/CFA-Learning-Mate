An analyst is evaluating the price sensitivity of the three bonds in Exhibit 1 to a parallel shift in the yield curve. The bond with the highest duration is most likely:

ABond A, the 10% annual coupon bond with five years to maturity
BBond B, the zero-coupon bond with five years to maturity
CBond C, the 5% coupon bond with two years to maturity
✓ B. Bond B, the zero-coupon bond with five years to maturity
✗ You chose C. Bond C, the 5% coupon bond with two years to maturity
Official Tips
✓ B

Correct. The zero-coupon bond has the highest duration because its entire cash flow occurs at maturity, maximizing the weighted average time to receipt.

✗ C

Incorrect. Although Bond C has a lower coupon than Bond A, its two-year maturity significantly reduces its duration relative to the five-year zero.

Why this answer
Duration is the weighted average time to receive a bond's cash flows — the later and smaller the interim coupons, the higher the duration.

All three bonds yield 6%, so duration is driven by coupon size and maturity:

\[ \text{Duration} = \frac{\sum_{t=1}^{n} t \cdot PV(CF_t)}{\sum_{t=1}^{n} PV(CF_t)} \]

Bond B pays nothing until maturity, so every cash flow is weighted at five years.

Why the others are wrong
  • A. The 10% coupon pulls cash flows earlier, lowering duration below the zero-coupon bond.
  • C. A low coupon raises duration, but the two-year maturity caps it well below the five-year zero.
Zero coupon = zero brakes. With nothing paid early, duration runs all the way to maturity.
Key terms
Macaulay Duration The weighted average time until a bond's cash flows are received, expressed in years.
Picture a see-saw: duration is the fulcrum point where all the coupon "weights" balance.
Modified Duration Macaulay duration divided by (1 + yield), giving direct price sensitivity per 1% yield change.
